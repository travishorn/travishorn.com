title: Fraud and Anomaly Detection Using Vectors
---

If you have a database of financial transactions and need to identify which ones
might be fraudulent, where do you begin? Your first instinct might be to write a
few simple threshold rules. You look for the most obvious red flags you can
think of and write a quick query to catch them:

```sql
SELECT
  t.*
FROM transactions t
WHERE
  t.amount > 5000
  AND t.country <> 'US';
```

There's a problem here, though. Criminals are rarely careless enough to make
transactions that get caught by such a naive check. They aren't stealing massive
amounts all at once. Instead, they try to blend in by making smaller
transactions at odd hours that won't trigger basic threshold alerts.

Our system needs to detect when a transaction *feels* out of place without
requiring us to write hundreds complex `if/else` rules.

The best part? You don't need a dedicated data science platform to do this.
Often, you can do it right inside the relational database you already use.

## Thinking in Dimensions

Let's start with a single property: **transaction amount**. We can plot a series
of transactions on a 1-dimensional number line.

![A number line starting at 0 and ending at 300. Dots are clustered on the left
with a few spaced far away on the right.](./images/1d-number-line.webp)

You can easily see a tight cluster of $5-$25 purchases, a couple of moderately
priced ones, and a single $300 outlier far off to the right.

Now let's add a second property: **time of day**. We can plot those exact same
transactions on a 2-dimensional grid.

![A scatter plot with numbers 0 to 300 on the X axis. Times 00:00 to 24:00 on
the Y axis. Dots are mostly clustered on the upper left with some
outliers.](./images/2d-flat-scatter.webp)

This gives us another dimension to visualize transaction patterns. Most are
low-cost and occur during the day, while only a few happen late at night or
carry higher price tags.

Let's take it a step further by adding a third property: **distance from home**.

![A 3D plot with axes for amount, time of day, and distance. Some dots are
clustered and others are outliers. Animated: the perspective slowly shifts to
see the dots from different angles.](./images/3d-scatter-transactions.webp)

Now our transactions are points floating in a 3D space. Regular transactions
cluster together: low-priced, daytime, and close to home. Outliers stand out
immediately: high-priced, late-night, distant purchases appear far away from the
core cluster.

Visually, three dimensions is our limit. Humans can't picture 4D or beyond, but
**the math doesn't change**.

Each extra property is just another axis added to our virtual graph.

You've likely heard of **vectors** or **vector databases**. A vector is simply a
point's address in that space. It's an array of numbers representing its
position along every single axis. For example: `[4.85, 7.45, 1.2, ...]`.

## From Normal Transactions to Vectors

Numeric properties are easy to plot and calculate distance between. Messy,
unstructured data like descriptions, memos, and merchant names are a different
story.

Luckily, we have **embedding models**. They take messy text and translate it
into vectors. Models like OpenAI's `text-embedding-3` are trained on a massive
amount of text. They can capture what a piece of text is *conceptually about*.
For instance, an embedding model places "Shell" and "Chevron" near each other
because, even though the strings look completely different, the model
understands they are both gas stations.

Storing vectors in your database is straightforward:

1. Add a column with the appropriate vector data type. In MariaDB, you can use
   `VECTOR(1536)`.
2. Pass the transaction's merchant name, description, memo, and other metadata
   to a local model or an API like OpenAI.
3. Save the returned array of numbers to the transaction record.

The vector lives right alongside the rest of your tabular transaction data.

## How "Normal" Behavior Clusters

A typical consumer's financial life forms a "neighborhood" in high-dimensional
space. They buy transit passes on weekday mornings. They frequent the same three
lunch spots. They pay for the same recurring monthly subscriptions.

Because your database can calculate the mathematical distance between points,
transactions that are conceptually similar will have very short distances
between them.

## Inverting Vector Search for Anomaly Detection

Most vector search use cases look for things that are close together. Anomaly
detection flips that logic around.

What happens when a new transaction arrives and its nearest neighbor is **miles
away**?

![A 3D scatter plot. Many points are grouped together. One point is far away.
That point is highlighted with a red color and
outline.](./images/3d-scatter-outlier.webp)

If a transaction lands in an empty, unpopulated region of a user's coordinate
space, it means **that user has never done anything conceptually like this
before**.

Consider a new transaction: $48 at 3:00 AM for a "cryptocurrency gift card
exchange."

At $48, it slips under the radar of standard alert rules. But in vector space,
it lands in an empty zone far away from the user's normal cluster.

That's an immediate anomaly flag.

## How It Looks in Practice

When a new transaction arrives, compute its vector and ask the database for the
distance to that user's closest past transaction:

```sql
SELECT
  VEC_DISTANCE_COSINE(embedding, :new_transaction_vector) AS distance
FROM transactions
WHERE
  user_id = :user_id
ORDER BY
  distance ASC
LIMIT 1;
```

If the nearest neighbor is further away than some safety threshold you set, you
can trigger a notification, pause the charge for review, or initiate some other
protections.

## Not Just a Gimmick

Vector technology isn't just for AI chatbots or search boxes. It's a practical,
mathematical tool for measuring context and spotting anomalies.

Take a look at your existing database tables. What other events create
"clusters" of regular behavior where outliers would be valuable to catch? Think
authentication logs, API usage patterns, customer support tickets, or user
reviews.
