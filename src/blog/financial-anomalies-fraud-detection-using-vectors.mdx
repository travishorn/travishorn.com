title: Financial Anomalies and Fraud Detection using Vectors
---

If you have a database of financial transactions and you need to identify which
ones might be fraudulent, how might you start? Here's a very simple query:

```sql
SELECT
  t.*
FROM transaction t
WHERE
  t.amount > 5000
  AND t.country <> 'US';
```

There's a problem here, though. Criminals committing fraud are rarely so
ignorant to make such an obvious transaction like that would be caught using a
naive query like that. They're not stealing large amounts in one transaction.
They attempt to avoid standing out by making smaller transactions at various
hours that don't trigger simple alerts.

Our system needs to detect when a transaction "feels" out of place without
writing hundreds of complex `if/else` rules.

You don't need a dedicated data science platform to do this! Often, you can do
it right inside the RDBMS you already use.

## Thinking in Dimensions

Let's consider a single property: **transaction amount**. We can plot some
transactions on a 1 dimensional number line.

![A number line starting at 0 and ending at 300. Dots are clustered on the left
with a few spaced far away on the right.](./images/1d-number-line.webp)

You can easily see a cluster of $5-25 transactions, a couple higher-prices
transactions, and one $300 transaction that's very far away from the others.

Now let's add another property: **time of day**. We can plot those exact same
transactions on a flat 2-dimension grid.

![A scatter plot with numbers 0 to 300 on the X axis. Times 00:00 to 24:00 on
the Y axis. Dots are mostly clustered on the upper left with some
outliers.](./images/2d-flat-scatter.webp)

This adds another dimension that we can use to further visualize clusters of
transactions. Most are low-price and during the day. Some are higher price
and/or later in the day.

Let's continue by adding a third property: **distance from home**.

![A 3D plot with axes for amount, time of day, and distance. Some dots are
clustered and others are outliers. Animated: the perspective slowly shifts to
see the dots from different angles.](./images/3d-scatter-transactions.webp)

Now transactions are floating points in a 3D cube. This gives us another
dimension to visually see which dots are clustered and which are outliers.
Regular transactions cluster together: low price, daytime, nearby. Outliers
appear easily because high price, nighttime, distant transactions appear further
from the regular cluster.


This is as far as we can go *visually*. Humans cannot picture 4D, 50D, or 768D
space, but **the math does not change**.

Each extra property (merchant category, device signature, memo keywords,
purchase frequency) is just another axis that we can add to our virtual graph.

You may have heard of **vectors** or a **vector database**. A vector is simply a
point's address. It's an array of numbers representing the point's position
along all those axes. For example: `[4.85, 7.45, 1.2, ...]`.

## From Normal Transactions to Vectors

Numeric properties are easy to plot and calculate a distance between. Messy,
complex properties like descriptions, memos, and behavior are a different story.

Luckily, we have **embedding models**. They take that messy data and translate
it into vectors. The models, like OpenAI's `text-embedding-3` have been trained
on an enormous amount of text and can easily capture what the transaction is
*conceptually about*. For example, an embedding model will place "Shell" and
"Chevron" near to each other because, even if those two words seem unrelated on
their face, we know (and the model knows) they are both names of gas stations.

Adding a vector is not complicated:

1. Add a database column with the appropriate data type. For example, in MariaDB, you can add `VECTOR(1536)`.
2. Pass a transaction's merchant name, description, memo, and similar to either a local model or a third-party like OpenAI.
3. Update the transaction with the returned vector.

The vector lives right alongside the rest of your transaction's values.

## How "Normal" Behavior Clusters

A typical consumer's financial life forms a "neighborhood" in high-dimensional
space. They may purchase transit passes every weekday. They may frequent nearby
lunch spots. They may pay for recurring subscriptions.

Your database system can calculate the distance between any two points. Points
that are near to each other have a high similarity.

## Inverting Vector Search for Anomaly Detection

What happens when you check a transaction and the distance to its nearest
neighbor is **miles away**?

If a new transaction lands in an empty, unpopulated region of the person's
coordinate space, it means **the person has never done anything conceptually
like this before**.

For example, let's say a new transaction arrives: $48 at 3:00 AM for a
"cryptocurrency gift card exchange".

The $48 amount is small enough to bypass traditional high-dollar alerts, but in
vector space, it is located completely outside her normal cluster.

That's an instant anomaly flag.

## How It Looks in Practice

When a new transaction arrives, find its distance to the same consumer's closest
past transactions:

```sql
SELECT
  VEC_DISTANCE_COSINE(embedding, :new_transaction_vector)
FROM transactions
WHERE
  user_id = :user_id
ORDER BY
  distance ASC
LIMIT 1;
```

If the resulting distance is beyond a certain threshold, send a notification or
pause the transaction for manual review.

## Not Just a Gimmick

Vector technology isn't just a gimmick for AI chatbots or search boxes. You can
use it to measure context and anomalous activity.

Look at your current database tables. What other data forms "clusters" where
outliers would be valuable to catch? Hint: think along the lines of error logs,
login events, and user review patterns.
